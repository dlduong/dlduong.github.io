How to use this course

The central task is to compute a posterior expectation

I=𝔼π[f(X)]=∫f(x)π(x)dx,I=\mathbb E_\pi[f(X)]=\int f(x)\pi(x)\,dx,

when the posterior density is available only up to a normalizing constant. MCMC constructs a stochastic process whose long-run occupation frequencies represent that posterior. We then use time averages to approximate the integral.

A running Bayesian inverse problem

Take a scalar unknown with a Gaussian prior and a noisy observation:

X∼𝒩(0,1),y=X+η,η∼𝒩(0,0.52),y=2.X\sim\mathcal N(0,1),\qquad y=X+\eta,\qquad \eta\sim\mathcal N(0,0.5^2),\qquad y=2.

Its posterior is

π(x)=Z−1exp[−x22−(2−x)22(0.5)2].\pi(x)=Z^{-1}\exp\left[-\frac{x^2}{2}-\frac{(2-x)^2}{2(0.5)^2}\right].

Completing the square gives

X∣y∼𝒩(1.6,0.2).X\mid y\sim\mathcal N(1.6,0.2).

Thus the exact posterior mean is 1.6, variance is 0.2, standard deviation is about 0.4472, and

ℙ(X>2∣y)≈0.18555.\mathbb P(X>2\mid y)\approx0.18555.

We could sample this posterior directly. We deliberately treat it as a target known only through its unnormalized log density, so every MCMC calculation has a checkable answer. For a nonlinear PDE inverse problem, the same structure becomes

π(x∣y)∝exp[−Φ(x;y)]π0(x),Φ(x;y)=12∥Γ−1/2(y−𝒢(x))∥2.\pi(x\mid y)\propto\exp[-\Phi(x;y)]\pi_0(x),\qquad \Phi(x;y)=\tfrac12\|\Gamma^{-1/2}(y-\mathcal G(x))\|^2.

Evaluating the log density may then require a PDE solve.

Part 1: Monte Carlo foundations (0-15 minutes)

1.1 Why high-dimensional numerical integration is difficult

Intuition. A tensor grid spends points resolving every coordinate combination, including regions that contribute little to the integral. Bayesian posteriors may concentrate on a thin, tilted, or curved region that the grid barely resolves.

Mathematics. A tensor rule with mm nodes in each of dd coordinates needs mdm^d evaluations. For a fixed-order rule with an error estimate proportional to hrh^r and N≍h−dN\asymp h^{-d}, this translates to O(N−r/d)O(N^{-r/d}), subject to its regularity assumptions. This is an illustration, not a lower bound for every integration method; sparse grids and other structure-exploiting methods can do much better on suitable problems.

Numerical example. With 20 nodes per coordinate, dimensions 1, 5, and 10 require 20, 3,200,000, and 10,240,000,000,000 evaluations. At one millisecond per evaluation, the last case takes about 325 years.

What problem does this solve? This motivates replacing full-domain resolution by evaluations at representative random points.

1.2 The curse of dimensionality

Intuition. Increasing dimension changes both computational cost and geometry. Regions that look large along one coordinate can occupy a tiny fraction of the full space.

Mathematics. A box with side length aa inside the unit cube has relative volume ada^d. If X∼𝒩(0,Id)X\sim\mathcal N(0,I_d), then

𝔼∥X∥2=d,Var(∥X∥2)=2d.\mathbb E\|X\|^2=d,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\|X\|^2)=2d.

For large dd, much of the probability lies in a shell with radius near d\sqrt d, even though the density is maximized at zero. Density height and probability mass are different quantities.

Numerical example. The central box [0.25,0.75]20[0.25,0.75]^{20} occupies only 2−20≈9.54×10−72^{-20}\approx9.54\times10^{-7} of the unit cube. For a 100-dimensional standard Gaussian, the typical radius is near 10; a sampler that merely finds the mode at zero has not explored the distribution.

What problem does this solve? We need a sampler to explore posterior mass, not just optimize the posterior density.

1.3 Monte Carlo estimation

Intuition. If points are drawn from the distribution appearing in the integral, their empirical frequencies already supply the integration weights.

Mathematics. For independent Xi∼πX_i\sim\pi,

ÎN=1N∑i=1Nf(Xi),𝔼ÎN=I,Var(ÎN)=Varπ(f)N,\widehat I_N=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N f(X_i),\qquad \mathbb E\widehat I_N=I,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\widehat I_N)=\frac{\operatorname{Var}_\pi(f)}{N},

where the variance identity assumes a finite second moment. To integrate with respect to Lebesgue measure over a bounded region DD, draw uniformly and multiply the sample mean by |D||D|.

Numerical example. Three hypothetical posterior draws, 1.2, 1.7, and 1.9, give a mean estimate of 1.6. Setting f(x)=𝟏{x>2}f(x)=\mathbf1_{\{x>2\}} makes the same average estimate a posterior event probability.

What problem does this solve? The final estimator in MCMC is also an empirical average. The challenge moves from integration to constructing useful samples.

Monte Carlo is a broader family than independent sampling: dependence is allowed when an appropriate law of large numbers holds. MCMC is one such construction.

1.4 Law of large numbers and Monte Carlo error

Intuition. More samples stabilize the average. But consistency and an error estimate are separate statements.

Mathematics. For IID samples and 𝔼π|f|<∞\mathbb E_\pi|f|<\infty, the strong law gives

ÎN→Ialmost surely.\widehat I_N\longrightarrow I\quad\text{almost surely}.

With finite variance σf2\sigma_f^2,

N(ÎN−I)⇒𝒩(0,σf2),MCSÊ=sf/N.\sqrt N(\widehat I_N-I)\Rightarrow\mathcal N(0,\sigma_f^2),\qquad \widehat{\operatorname{MCSE}}=s_f/\sqrt N.

An approximate 95% Monte Carlo interval for the integral is ÎN±1.96MCSE\widehat I_N\pm1.96\operatorname{MCSE}.

Numerical example. For the running posterior mean, N=10,000N=10,000 independent draws give MCSE 0.2/10,000≈0.00447\sqrt{0.2/10,000}\approx0.00447. Reducing this to about 0.00224 requires 40,000 draws.

What problem does this solve? MCSE quantifies numerical uncertainty in an estimated expectation. The posterior standard deviation, 0.4472, quantifies uncertainty about XX given the data. The former shrinks with simulation length; the latter does not.

The N−1/2N^{-1/2} rate has no explicit dimension exponent. Its variance constant, sampling cost, and required accuracy can nevertheless deteriorate with dimension. It is not a universal escape from dimensionality.

1.5 Importance sampling

Intuition. If sampling from π\pi is difficult, sample from a convenient proposal qq and correct the mismatch using weights. Draws in important but undersampled regions receive large weights.

Mathematics. If the normalized density is known and qq covers its support,

I=𝔼q[f(X)π(X)q(X)].I=\mathbb E_q\left[f(X)\frac{\pi(X)}{q(X)}\right].

For π=π̃/Z\pi=\widetilde\pi/Z with unknown ZZ, use self-normalized importance sampling:

Xi∼iidq,wi=π̃(Xi)q(Xi),ÎSNIS=∑iwif(Xi)∑iwi.X_i\overset{\mathrm{iid}}\sim q,\quad w_i=\frac{\widetilde\pi(X_i)}{q(X_i)},\qquad \widehat I_{\mathrm{SNIS}}=\frac{\sum_iw_if(X_i)}{\sum_iw_i}.

This ratio estimator is generally biased at finite NN, but consistent under suitable integrability and support assumptions. Its variance can be very large or infinite. A common weight-concentration diagnostic is

ESSIS=(∑iwi)2∑iwi2.\operatorname{ESS}_{\mathrm{IS}}=\frac{(\sum_iw_i)^2}{\sum_iw_i^2}.

Numerical example. Use our prior q=𝒩(0,1)q=\mathcal N(0,1). Then w(x)w(x) is proportional to exp[−2(2−x)2]\exp[-2(2-x)^2]. Hypothetical draws 0,1,20,1,2 have weights e−8,e−2,1e^{-8},e^{-2},1, giving a weighted mean about 1.880, compared with the unweighted mean 1. The weight ESS is only about 1.27 out of 3.

What problem does this solve? Importance sampling is a valid alternative to MCMC when a good global proposal exists. It also shows that an unknown normalizer alone is not sufficient to make a Bayesian computation impossible.

1.6 Why straightforward Monte Carlo often struggles in Bayesian problems

Intuition. We can often evaluate a posterior density but cannot draw independent samples from it. Prior draws may almost never visit the concentrated posterior region, and importance weighting cannot recover regions never visited.

Mathematics. For q=𝒩(0,Id)q=\mathcal N(0,I_d) and π=𝒩(a𝟏,Id)\pi=\mathcal N(a\mathbf1,I_d), the normalized importance weight satisfies

𝔼qw=1,𝔼qw2=eda2.\mathbb E_qw=1,\qquad \mathbb E_qw^2=e^{da^2}.

Thus the large-sample weight ESS fraction is e−da2e^{-da^2}, when the corresponding empirical moments are adequately resolved.

Numerical example. For a=1a=1 and d=10d=10, this fraction is e−10≈4.54×10−5e^{-10}\approx4.54\times10^{-5}. The asymptotic expression predicts about 45 effective draws per million proposals; a particular finite run can behave much worse or give a misleading ESS estimate.

What problem does this solve? MCMC trades difficult independent global sampling for a sequence of simpler transitions. It can explore adaptively through the state space, at the price of dependence and initialization effects. It can still struggle with concentration, expensive forward solves, and separated modes.

Pause: the next question is: can we design dependent samples whose long-run frequencies are exactly the posterior frequencies?

Part 2: Markov chains (15-30 minutes)

2.1 The Markov property

Intuition. The present state contains all the information needed to generate the next state. The next state may still be strongly dependent on the present state.

Mathematics. For measurable AA,

ℙ(Xn+1∈A∣X0,…,Xn)=ℙ(Xn+1∈A∣Xn).\mathbb P(X_{n+1}\in A\mid X_0,\ldots,X_n)=\mathbb P(X_{n+1}\in A\mid X_n).

For a time-homogeneous chain this conditional distribution is described by one kernel PP, independent of nn.

Numerical example. Consider states 0,10,1 and transition matrix

P=(0.80.20.10.9).P=\begin{pmatrix}0.8&0.2\\0.1&0.9\end{pmatrix}.

If the current state is 0, the next state is 1 with probability 0.2, regardless of how 0 was reached. Consecutive values are not independent because the probability is 0.9 when the current state is 1.

What problem does this solve? A sampler becomes a rule that takes the current state and fresh randomness and returns the next state.

2.2 Transition kernels

Intuition. A kernel specifies an entire distribution of possible next states for each current state.

Mathematics. P(x,A)P(x,A) is a probability measure in AA and measurable in xx. A current distribution μ\mu evolves according to

(μP)(A)=∫P(x,A)μ(dx). (\mu P)(A)=\int P(x,A)\,\mu(dx).

Two steps use

P2(x,A)=∫P(z,A)P(x,dz).P^2(x,A)=\int P(z,A)P(x,dz).

For an accept/reject sampler with proposal density qq,

P(x,dz)=q(z∣x)α(x,z)dz+r(x)δx(dz),r(x)=1−∫q(z∣x)α(x,z)dz.P(x,dz)=q(z\mid x)\alpha(x,z)\,dz+r(x)\delta_x(dz),\qquad r(x)=1-\int q(z\mid x)\alpha(x,z)\,dz.

The atom at xx records rejection.

Numerical example. In the two-state chain, P2(0,0)=0.82+0.2(0.1)=0.66P^2(0,0)=0.8^2+0.2(0.1)=0.66: return via 0 or via 1.

What problem does this solve? Kernel notation lets us prove correctness of an algorithm without analyzing every possible simulated trajectory. A proposal kernel and the resulting accepted-chain kernel are different objects.

2.3 Stationary distributions

Intuition. A stationary distribution is unchanged by one transition. If a chain starts with that distribution, every subsequent state has it too.

Mathematics. π\pi is invariant, or stationary, if

πP=π,π(A)=∫P(x,A)π(dx).\pi P=\pi,\qquad \pi(A)=\int P(x,A)\pi(dx).

If X0∼πX_0\sim\pi, then Xn∼πX_n\sim\pi for every nn, although the XnX_n need not be independent.

Numerical example. Our matrix has stationary probabilities (1/3,2/3)(1/3,2/3) because (1/3)(0.2)=(2/3)(0.1)(1/3)(0.2)=(2/3)(0.1). But starting deterministically at 0 does not start in stationarity.

What problem does this solve? The first correctness requirement is to make the posterior invariant. This alone is insufficient: the identity kernel P(x,⋅)=δxP(x,\cdot)=\delta_x preserves every distribution and never explores.

Do not confuse time homogeneity (the transition rule does not change) with stationarity (the law of the process is at equilibrium).

2.4 Ergodicity

Intuition. The chain must actually explore the target rather than stay in a closed region or retain permanent dependence on its starting point.

Mathematics. For a finite state space, irreducibility plus aperiodicity gives convergence to the unique invariant distribution. On general state spaces, positive Harris recurrence plus aperiodicity is a standard sufficient framework for total-variation convergence and, for integrable ff, the ergodic law

1N∑n=1Nf(Xn)→∫fdπa.s.\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^Nf(X_n)\longrightarrow\int f\,d\pi\quad\text{a.s.}

Here irreducibility means that all regions of positive target measure are accessible; Harris recurrence rules out permanent avoidance of such regions; aperiodicity rules out a compulsory cycle. Precise starting-state qualifications depend on the theorem used.

Numerical example. Starting our two-state chain at 0,

ℙ(Xn=1)=23(1−0.7n).\mathbb P(X_n=1)=\frac23(1-0.7^n).

At n=10n=10 this is approximately 0.6478, approaching 2/32/3. In contrast, a chain that deterministically alternates 0 and 1 has an invariant uniform distribution but an oscillating one-step marginal from a fixed start. Its time average still converges: convergence of marginals and convergence of averages are distinct statements.

What problem does this solve? Ergodic theorems justify MCMC averages. A central limit theorem and a finite asymptotic variance require additional assumptions; an invariant distribution or a law of large numbers alone does not supply an MCSE.

2.5 Detailed balance

Intuition. At equilibrium, the probability flow from one region to another equals the reverse flow. This is a convenient way to enforce invariance.

Mathematics. Reversibility with respect to π\pi means

π(dx)P(x,dz)=π(dz)P(z,dx).\pi(dx)P(x,dz)=\pi(dz)P(z,dx).

Integrating over xx gives πP=π\pi P=\pi. Detailed balance is sufficient, not necessary, for stationarity.

Numerical example. In our chain, the equilibrium flow 0→10\to1 is (1/3)(0.2)=1/15(1/3)(0.2)=1/15, exactly the reverse flow (2/3)(0.1)(2/3)(0.1).

What problem does this solve? Metropolis-Hastings constructs an acceptance probability that makes these flows agree. Detailed balance does not imply rapid mixing or even irreducibility.

Part 3: MCMC algorithms (30-52 minutes)

3.1 The Metropolis algorithm

Intuition. Make a symmetric proposal. Always accept moves to a higher-density point; accept some moves to lower density. Those downward moves are essential for sampling uncertainty rather than just climbing to a mode.

Mathematics. With q(z∣x)=q(x∣z)q(z\mid x)=q(x\mid z),

Z∼q(⋅∣x),α(x,Z)=min{1,π̃(Z)π̃(x)}.Z\sim q(\cdot\mid x),\qquad \alpha(x,Z)=\min\left\{1,\frac{\widetilde\pi(Z)}{\widetilde\pi(x)}\right\}.

Draw U∼Unif(0,1)U\sim\operatorname{Unif}(0,1) and set the next state to ZZ if U<αU<\alpha; otherwise retain xx. The unknown normalizer cancels in the ratio.

Numerical example. In our Gaussian posterior, propose z=2.1z=2.1 from x=1.6x=1.6. Then

α=exp[−(2.1−1.6)22(0.2)]=e−0.625≈0.5353.\alpha=\exp\left[-\frac{(2.1-1.6)^2}{2(0.2)}\right]=e^{-0.625}\approx0.5353.

Accept if U=0.3U=0.3; reject if U=0.8U=0.8. A proposal from 2.1 back to 1.6 is accepted with probability 1.

What problem does this solve? This is the smallest useful posterior sampler. After rejection, record the repeated state. Keeping only accepted states generally changes the stationary distribution of the recorded sequence.

3.2 Metropolis-Hastings

Intuition. An asymmetric proposal may favor one direction. Correct for that asymmetry so the accepted chain has the right equilibrium frequencies.

Mathematics. For target and proposal densities with the appropriate support,

α(x,z)=min{1,π̃(z)q(x∣z)π̃(x)q(z∣x)}.\alpha(x,z)=\min\left\{1, \frac{\widetilde\pi(z)q(x\mid z)}{\widetilde\pi(x)q(z\mid x)}\right\}.

For a move with positive forward proposal density and zero reverse density, the acceptance probability is zero. Initialize at a state with positive target density. The off-diagonal detailed-balance identity is

π(x)q(z∣x)α(x,z)=min{π(x)q(z∣x),π(z)q(x∣z)},\pi(x)q(z\mid x)\alpha(x,z)=\min\{\pi(x)q(z\mid x),\pi(z)q(x\mid z)\},

which is symmetric in x,zx,z. The rejection term lies on the diagonal and also satisfies balance.

Numerical example. Let π(0)=0.2\pi(0)=0.2, π(1)=0.8\pi(1)=0.8, with q(1∣0)=0.9q(1\mid0)=0.9 and q(0∣1)=0.3q(0\mid1)=0.3. Then

α(0,1)=1,α(1,0)=0.75.\alpha(0,1)=1,\qquad \alpha(1,0)=0.75.

The accepted probability flows agree: 0.2(0.9)=0.8(0.3)(0.75)=0.180.2(0.9)=0.8(0.3)(0.75)=0.18. Ignoring the proposal ratio would give the wrong invariant distribution.

What problem does this solve? MH is a general correction mechanism. Random walks, independence proposals, gradient-based proposals, and many other designs fit this framework.

3.3 Gibbs sampling

Intuition. Sampling the joint distribution may be hard while sampling one conditional distribution at a time is easy. Update coordinates or blocks using their exact full conditionals.

Mathematics. For two variables, one systematic sweep is

X1n+1∼π(x1∣X2n),X2n+1∼π(x2∣X1n+1).X_1^{n+1}\sim\pi(x_1\mid X_2^n),\qquad X_2^{n+1}\sim\pi(x_2\mid X_1^{n+1}).

Each conditional-update kernel preserves π\pi; therefore their composition preserves π\pi. An exact full-conditional update is also an MH update with acceptance probability 1. A systematic full sweep need not be reversible, illustrating that detailed balance is not necessary.

Numerical example. For

(X1X2)∼𝒩(0,(1ρρ1)),\begin{pmatrix}X_1\\X_2\end{pmatrix}\sim\mathcal N\left(0, \begin{pmatrix}1&\rho\\\rho&1\end{pmatrix}\right),

the conditionals are X1∣X2=x2∼𝒩(ρx2,1−ρ2)X_1\mid X_2=x_2\sim\mathcal N(\rho x_2,1-\rho^2) and similarly for X2∣X1X_2\mid X_1. With ρ=0.9\rho=0.9 and current x2=1x_2=1, draw X1∼𝒩(0.9,0.19)X_1\sim\mathcal N(0.9,0.19). A standard-normal innovation of -1 gives x1≈0.4641x_1\approx0.4641; a zero innovation in the second update gives x2≈0.4177x_2\approx0.4177.

What problem does this solve? Gibbs is attractive for conditional conjugacy and hierarchical models. Strong correlations can make coordinate updates very slow despite 100% acceptance. If exact conditionals are unavailable, MH updates inside a Gibbs sweep give Metropolis-within-Gibbs.

3.4 Random-walk proposals

Intuition. Propose a random perturbation of the current point. Small steps are accepted often but move slowly; large steps travel farther but are rejected more frequently.

Mathematics. A common proposal is

Z=X+sLξ,ξ∼𝒩(0,Id),LL⊤=Σ,Z=X+sL\xi,\qquad \xi\sim\mathcal N(0,I_d),\qquad LL^\top=\Sigma,

with fixed LL and ss. This is symmetric. Here ss is a step scale and Σ\Sigma describes the direction-dependent shape. Choosing a shape aligned with the posterior can improve exploration greatly.

Numerical example. Our posterior has standard deviation 0.4472. A proposal scale s=0.02s=0.02 is tiny; s=1s=1 makes substantial moves; s=5s=5 often proposes far outside the main posterior region. For a Gaussian target in dd dimensions, an isotropic proposal perturbation has expected squared length ds2ds^2; retaining useful acceptance generally requires shrinking the per-coordinate scale as dimension grows.

What problem does this solve? Random-walk Metropolis is a useful reference implementation. It exposes the central efficiency tradeoff and can work when gradients are unavailable, but often struggles under high-dimensional mesh refinement.

First implementation: a correct random-walk Metropolis sampler

Use log densities to avoid numerical underflow. This code counts and stores every post-warmup transition, including rejections.

import numpy as np

def log_target(x):
    return -0.5*x*x - 0.5*((2.0-x)/0.5)**2

def rwm(logp, x0, scale, draws=10_000, warmup=2_000, seed=42):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    x = float(x0)
    lp = float(logp(x))
    if not np.isfinite(lp):
        raise ValueError("Initial state must have finite log density")
    samples = np.empty(draws)
    accepted = 0
    for t in range(warmup + draws):
        z = x + scale*rng.normal()
        lz = float(logp(z))
        if np.isnan(lz) or np.isposinf(lz):
            raise ValueError("Invalid proposed log density")
        take = np.log(rng.uniform()) < min(0.0, lz-lp)
        if take:
            x, lp = z, lz
        if t >= warmup:
            samples[t-warmup] = x
            accepted += int(take)
    return samples, accepted/draws

chains = np.stack([
    rwm(log_target, x0, scale=1.0, seed=100+j)[0]
    for j, x0 in enumerate([-3.0, 0.0, 3.0, 6.0])
])
print(chains.mean(), chains.var(ddof=1))  # near 1.6 and 0.2

The returned acceptance rate covers retained transitions only. This teaching implementation does not adapt its scale during warmup. The attached Python package includes uncertainty estimates, plots, Gibbs, fixed-length HMC, and automated checks against exact Gaussian answers.

Pause: correctness of the transition rule is only half the problem. We must now assess the finite run.

Part 4: Practical MCMC (52-70 minutes)

4.1 Burn-in and warmup

Intuition. A chain initialized far from the posterior may spend early iterations approaching it. Adaptive samplers also use an initial phase to learn numerical tuning parameters.

Mathematics. Discarding BB iterations gives

ÎB,N=1N∑n=B+1B+Nf(Xn),𝔼ÎB,N−I=1N∑n=B+1B+N[μ0Pnf−πf].\widehat I_{B,N}=\frac1N\sum_{n=B+1}^{B+N}f(X_n),\qquad \mathbb E\widehat I_{B,N}-I=\frac1N\sum_{n=B+1}^{B+N}\big[\mu_0P^nf-\pi f\big].

Burn-in aims to reduce initialization bias. Warmup can additionally adapt proposal scales or an HMC metric. In the simple workflow, adaptation ends before the retained run; continuing arbitrary adaptation can invalidate the fixed-kernel theory.

Numerical example. Start the scalar chain at -3 although its posterior mean is 1.6. The first segment may trend upward. Dropping 2,000 iterations is an experimental choice, not a theorem that all later draws are stationary. Starting at the posterior mean also does not produce the posterior distribution.

What problem does this solve? Use a warmup phase, initialize several chains at meaningfully dispersed plausible states, and diagnose the retained phase. Removing more draws cannot make a chain cross a barrier it never crosses.

4.2 Mixing

Intuition. Mixing describes how rapidly a chain forgets its start and explores different parts of the target. A correct sampler can mix so slowly that its finite output is unusable.

Mathematics. One theoretical measure is

∥Pn(x,⋅)−π∥TV.\|P^n(x,\cdot)-\pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}}.

For reversible chains, spectral properties of PP on centered L2(π)L^2(\pi) functions help quantify convergence and autocorrelation. In practice, efficiency also depends on the particular function ff being estimated.

Numerical example. Consider a mixture 12𝒩(−5,1)+12𝒩(5,1)\tfrac12\mathcal N(-5,1)+\tfrac12\mathcal N(5,1). A small-step random walk can explore one mode thoroughly and fail to reach the other in a long finite run. Its histogram may look smooth while its estimated mean is near -5 or 5, instead of the correct 0.

What problem does this solve? Poor mixing calls for a better proposal, reparameterization, blocking, tempering, or another algorithm. A longer run helps only if its length is adequate relative to the actual exploration timescale.

4.3 Autocorrelation

Intuition. Adjacent draws often repeat similar information. The relevant dependence is the dependence of the quantity f(Xn)f(X_n), not just of each raw parameter.

Mathematics. For a stationary chain with finite variance, define

ρf(k)=Covπ(f(X0),f(Xk))σf2.\rho_f(k)=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}_\pi(f(X_0),f(X_k))}{\sigma_f^2}.

Then the exact finite-NN identity is

Var(ÎN)=σf2N[1+2∑k=1N−1(1−kN)ρf(k)].\operatorname{Var}(\widehat I_N)=\frac{\sigma_f^2}{N}\left[1+2\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\left(1-\frac{k}{N}\right)\rho_f(k)\right].

Numerical example. A stationary AR(1) chain

Xn+1=μ+a(Xn−μ)+σ1−a2ξnX_{n+1}=\mu+a(X_n-\mu)+\sigma\sqrt{1-a^2}\,\xi_n

has invariant distribution 𝒩(μ,σ2)\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2) and ρ(k)=ak\rho(k)=a^k. With a=0.9a=0.9, lag-1 correlation is 0.9 and lag-10 correlation is approximately 0.349.

What problem does this solve? Autocorrelation explains why the IID standard error formula is too optimistic for positively correlated MCMC output.

4.4 Effective sample size and Monte Carlo standard error

Intuition. ESS expresses the variance of a correlated average in units of independent draws. It is a property of an estimator, not a count of literally independent states hidden inside the output.

Mathematics. When the relevant CLT and summability conditions hold,

τf=1+2∑k=1∞ρf(k),Neff,f≈Nτf,MCSE(ÎN)≈σf2Neff,f.\tau_f=1+2\sum_{k=1}^\infty\rho_f(k),\qquad N_{{\mathrm{eff}},f}\approx\frac{N}{\tau_f},\qquad \operatorname{MCSE}(\widehat I_N)\approx\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_f^2}{N_{{\mathrm{eff}},f}}}.

The Markov chain CLT has asymptotic variance σf2τf\sigma_f^2\tau_f. Negative correlations can give ESS greater than NN. A chain may have different ESS values for XX, X2X^2, an event indicator, and a predictive quantity. The importance-weight ESS from Part 1 measures a different source of inefficiency.

Numerical example. For the AR(1) example,

τ=1+a1−a=19(a=0.9).\tau=\frac{1+a}{1-a}=19\quad(a=0.9).

Thus 10,000 draws have ESS about 526 for the mean. With posterior variance 0.2, MCSE is about 0.0195, over four times the IID value 0.00447.

What problem does this solve? Decide simulation length from the MCSE needed for your scientific quantity. If a mean MCSE of 0.01 is required here, target ESS of at least 0.2/0.012=2,0000.2/0.01^2=2,000, after resolving convergence concerns. Use established autocorrelation or spectral-variance estimators; summing every noisy empirical ACF lag is unstable. Routine thinning usually discards useful information and does not fix mixing; retain draws unless storage or downstream cost justifies thinning.

4.5 Acceptance rates

Intuition. Acceptance tells you how often a proposal is used. It does not tell you whether accepted moves travel far enough to explore the target.

Mathematics. For NN retained transitions,

â=#{accepted proposals}N.\widehat a=\frac{\#\{\text{accepted proposals}\}}{N}.

An efficiency objective is ESS per second or per forward/gradient evaluation. Expected squared jumping distance, 𝔼∥Xn+1−Xn∥2\mathbb E\|X_{n+1}-X_n\|^2, is another useful proxy in suitable settings.

Numerical example. For a stationary scalar Gaussian target with standard deviation σ\sigma and a Gaussian random-walk step of standard deviation ss, the exact mean acceptance is

a(s)=2πarctan(2σs).a(s)=\frac2\pi\arctan\left(\frac{2\sigma}{s}\right).

Using σ=0.2\sigma=\sqrt{0.2}, scales 0.05, 1, and 5 give acceptance about 0.964, 0.465, and 0.113. The first has the highest acceptance but takes tiny steps. Exact Gibbs updates always accept and can still mix poorly.

What problem does this solve? Use acceptance as a tuning signal alongside ESS and plots. The famous 0.234 rule is an asymptotic optimum for particular high-dimensional random-walk target families and efficiency criteria; it is not a universal target. HMC acceptance and the NUTS acceptance statistic have different tuning behavior [1, 2].

An executed experiment with four chains and 10,000 retained draws per chain gave:

Step scale Acceptance Estimated mean Batch estimate of mean ESS Modern R-hat
0.05 0.965 1.5848 513 1.0529
1 0.466 1.5950 9,287 1.0010
5 0.113 1.5901 3,572 1.0021

All runs retained 40,000 states in total. The slow run fails the R-hat screening check; its batch ESS and MCSE are provisional and can be optimistic because the batches are short relative to its dependence. The important comparison is that the highest acceptance did not produce the most useful sample.

4.6 Trace plots

Intuition. Plot values in iteration order to see trends, sticking, and disagreement across chains. A histogram throws away time order and can hide these problems.

Mathematics. A trace plot is the set of ordered pairs {(n,f(Xn))}\{(n,f(X_n))\}. Inspect several chains overlaid or in aligned panels. For a Gaussian target, nearly all posterior draws lie within about μ±3σ\mu\pm3\sigma, although occasional excursions are expected.

Numerical example. Our posterior has μ±3σ≈[0.258,2.942]\mu\pm3\sigma\approx[0.258,2.942]. Chains that repeatedly explore this range and overlap are more reassuring than one stuck near 1.5 and another near 2.5. But a plausible-looking trace restricted to one mixture mode can still be misleading.

What problem does this solve? Inspect traces of parameters, log density, and scientific quantities. Pair plots reveal correlations; rank plots compare how chains occupy the pooled distribution. Visual checks supplement quantitative diagnostics, without certifying convergence.

4.7 Convergence diagnostics

Intuition. Independent chains started in different places should eventually produce compatible distributions. Diagnostics look for evidence that they have not done so. Agreement cannot reveal a region every chain missed.

Mathematics. For MM chains of length nn, define within-chain variance WW, chain means x‾j\bar x_j, grand mean x‾\bar x, and

B=nM−1∑j=1M(x‾j−x‾)2,V̂=n−1nW+Bn,R̂classical=V̂/W.B=\frac{n}{M-1}\sum_{j=1}^M(\bar x_j-\bar x)^2,\qquad \widehat V=\frac{n-1}{n}W+\frac{B}{n},\qquad \widehat R_{\mathrm{classical}}=\sqrt{\widehat V/W}.

This explains the comparison. Modern practice uses rank-normalized split and folded versions, which detect more failures than the classical formula [3]. Bulk ESS summarizes efficiency in the center of the distribution; tail ESS targets tail/quantile behavior. For a particular posterior mean, estimate its own MCSE rather than interpreting rank-based bulk ESS as its exact variance ESS.

Numerical example. Suppose n=1,000n=1,000, W=0.2W=0.2, and B=20B=20. The classical value is (0.999⋅0.2+0.02)/0.2≈1.048\sqrt{(0.999\cdot0.2+0.02)/0.2}\approx1.048, indicating disagreement. A reported modern R̂=1.001\widehat R=1.001 is reassuring but does not prove all modes were visited.

What problem does this solve? A practical starting workflow is four chains, modern R̂\widehat R near 1 (typically below 1.01), adequate bulk and tail ESS, and an MCSE small enough for the scientific decision. Numerical thresholds are screening conventions, not correctness proofs [4]. For HMC, also investigate divergences, repeated maximum tree-depth events, and energy diagnostics. A divergence is a failure of reliable numerical trajectory integration in a region of the posterior, and can signal biased exploration [2].

For a PDE problem, keep two errors separate: MCMC error relative to the discretized posterior and discretization error relative to the intended continuum posterior. More chain iterations reduce only the former.

A practical end-to-end workflow

  1. Define the posterior and the scientific quantities ff.
  2. Implement a stable unnormalized log density; verify it on a solvable example.
  3. Choose a sampler and parameterization; evaluate gradient and forward-solve costs where relevant.
  4. Run several chains with warmup. Freeze ordinary tuning before retaining samples.
  5. Inspect traces, modern R̂\widehat R, ESS, and sampler-specific diagnostics.
  6. Fix exploration problems before trusting an apparently small MCSE.
  7. Estimate posterior quantities and their MCSE; extend a trustworthy run if numerical precision is insufficient.
  8. Report the model/discretization, sampler, tuning, retained draws, diagnostics, and accuracy relevant to the reported quantities.

Part 5: Modern perspective (70-80 minutes)

5.1 Hamiltonian Monte Carlo

Intuition. A random walk chooses directions without using posterior geometry. HMC introduces momentum and follows a dynamical trajectory that moves through the posterior while approximately preserving a suitable energy. Momentum is refreshed to create stochastic exploration.

Mathematics. Write U(x)=−logπ̃(x)U(x)=-\log\widetilde\pi(x) and introduce momentum p∼𝒩(0,M)p\sim\mathcal N(0,M) with a fixed positive-definite mass matrix. The extended target and Hamiltonian are

π¯(x,p)∝e−H(x,p),H(x,p)=U(x)+12p⊤M−1p.\overline\pi(x,p)\propto e^{-H(x,p)},\qquad H(x,p)=U(x)+\tfrac12p^\top M^{-1}p.

The xx marginal is the desired posterior. Hamilton’s equations are

ẋ=M−1p,ṗ=−∇U(x).\dot x=M^{-1}p,\qquad \dot p=-\nabla U(x).

A leapfrog step with size ϵ\epsilon is

p1/2=p−ϵ2∇U(x),x′=x+ϵM−1p1/2,p′=p1/2−ϵ2∇U(x′).p_{1/2}=p-\tfrac\epsilon2\nabla U(x),\qquad x'=x+\epsilon M^{-1}p_{1/2},\qquad p'=p_{1/2}-\tfrac\epsilon2\nabla U(x').

Compose LL steps and apply the MH correction

α=min{1,eH(x,p)−H(x′,p′)}.\alpha=\min\{1,e^{H(x,p)-H(x',p')}\}.

The standard proposal includes a final momentum flip, making reversibility explicit; the kinetic energy is unchanged by that flip. Leapfrog’s volume preservation means there is no Jacobian correction. An arbitrary ODE solver cannot simply be substituted with the same justification [2].

Numerical example. For our target, take U(x)=2.5(x−1.6)2U(x)=2.5(x-1.6)^2, M=1M=1, x=1.6x=1.6, p=1p=1, and ϵ=0.2\epsilon=0.2. One leapfrog step gives p1/2=1p_{1/2}=1, x′=1.8x'=1.8, p′=0.9p'=0.9. The energy increases from 0.5 to 0.505, so acceptance is e−0.005≈0.9950e^{-0.005}\approx0.9950. The move travels about 0.45 posterior standard deviations with very high acceptance.

What problem does this solve? HMC can make long, directed moves with high acceptance on smooth continuous targets. It needs gradients, a suitable parameterization, a step size, a mass matrix, and an integration length. In PDE inverse problems, gradients may require adjoint solves. Compare ESS per computational cost, not just iterations. HMC is a sampling method; its trajectories are not gradient descent toward the MAP.

5.2 The No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS)

Intuition. A short HMC trajectory barely moves; an excessively long one can turn back and waste work. NUTS constructs trajectories and selects states using a rule that avoids requiring a fixed hand-tuned trajectory length.

Mathematics. A simplified endpoint no-U-turn check uses endpoints (x−,p−)(x^-,p^-) and (x+,p+)(x^+,p^+) and displacement Δ=x+−x−\Delta=x^+-x^-:

Δ⊤M−1p−≥0,Δ⊤M−1p+≥0.\Delta^\top M^{-1}p^-\ge0,\qquad \Delta^\top M^{-1}p^+\ge0.

Failure of a condition suggests turning back. Actual NUTS algorithms use a trajectory tree, internal stopping checks, and carefully designed state-selection rules. Stopping ordinary HMC at an arbitrary state-dependent time and returning its endpoint is not automatically valid [5].

Numerical example. In the scalar quadratic example with M=1M=1, exact trajectories oscillate with angular frequency 5\sqrt5 and period 2π/5≈2.812\pi/\sqrt5\approx2.81. Starting at the mean with positive momentum, the first turning point occurs at time π/(25)≈0.702\pi/(2\sqrt5)\approx0.702. A fixed integration time near a whole period can return close to the starting point. This illustrates the problem; it is not a formula for NUTS’s actual stopping time.

What problem does this solve? During warmup, modern implementations adapt step size and mass matrix. During sampling, the trajectory length still varies from iteration to iteration according to a fixed valid NUTS transition rule. State-dependent trajectory length is different from continuing unrestricted adaptation of tuning parameters [2, 5].

5.3 Why Stan and PyMC favor HMC/NUTS

Intuition. Smooth multivariate posteriors often have correlated, unevenly scaled geometry. Gradients guide longer moves, and mass-matrix adaptation improves global scaling. This usually extracts more information per retained draw than a simple isotropic random walk, although each iteration costs more.

Mathematics. A random walk with steps of scale ss travels a root-mean-square distance of order sns\sqrt n after nn uncorrelated increments. Along a well-resolved Hamiltonian trajectory, displacement initially accumulates coherently over many leapfrog steps, before curvature turns it around. This is a geometric explanation, not a universal complexity theorem.

Numerical example. In a correlated Gaussian target with correlation 0.99, exact two-coordinate Gibbs sampling has lag-1 correlation 0.992=0.98010.99^2=0.9801 for a coordinate observed once per complete sweep. Its integrated autocorrelation time is about

1+0.98011−0.9801≈99.5.\frac{1+0.9801}{1-0.9801}\approx99.5.

So 10,000 full sweeps provide only about 100 effective draws for that coordinate’s mean, despite every update being accepted. Geometry-aware methods and reparameterization can substantially improve this situation.

What problem does this solve? Stan uses NUTS by default; PyMC generally uses NUTS for compatible continuous differentiable models and offers other samplers for other cases [2, 6]. Gradients do not solve every problem: discontinuities, discrete variables, funnels, separated modes, and expensive PDE derivatives can require different methods or substantial reformulation.

A minimal PyMC version of the running example is:

import pymc as pm
import arviz as az

with pm.Model() as model:
    x = pm.Normal("x", mu=0.0, sigma=1.0)
    y = pm.Normal("y", mu=x, sigma=0.5, observed=2.0)
    idata = pm.sample(
        draws=2_000, tune=1_000, chains=4,
        random_seed=42, target_accept=0.9,
    )

print(az.summary(idata, var_names=["x"]))
az.plot_trace(idata, var_names=["x"])

tune specifies warmup draws, which are normally discarded. target_accept=0.9 is an HMC/NUTS step-size tuning choice, not a statement that the resulting sample is reliable. Inspect the diagnostics. The PyMC example is supplied as an optional notebook section; PyMC is not required for the independently implemented and executed NumPy examples.

Bridge to function-space Bayesian inverse problems

For your research, an important next method is preconditioned Crank-Nicolson (pCN). It avoids starting from a fictitious infinite-dimensional Lebesgue density. For a Gaussian prior μ0=𝒩(0,C)\mu_0=\mathcal N(0,C) and posterior

dμydμ0(u)∝e−Φ(u;y),\frac{d\mu^y}{d\mu_0}(u)\propto e^{-\Phi(u;y)},

the proposal

v=1−β2u+βξ,ξ∼μ0,v=\sqrt{1-\beta^2}\,u+\beta\xi,\qquad \xi\sim\mu_0,

is reversible with respect to the prior. Its acceptance probability is

α(u,v)=min{1,eΦ(u;y)−Φ(v;y)}.\alpha(u,v)=\min\{1,e^{\Phi(u;y)-\Phi(v;y)}\}.

For example, Φ(u)=3\Phi(u)=3 and Φ(v)=3.7\Phi(v)=3.7 give acceptance e−0.7≈0.497e^{-0.7}\approx0.497. If the likelihood is constant, every proposal is accepted and the Gaussian prior is invariant. This gives a direct connection between MH and continuum Bayesian inverse problems. Prior preservation addresses a key source of mesh-refinement deterioration; it does not guarantee fast mixing for every likelihood [7].

The downloadable examples include pCN for a Gaussian inverse problem with coordinate-dependent prior variances, allowing comparison against exact posterior moments.

Part 6: The Bayesian inverse problem workflow (80-87 minutes)

6.1 Construct the likelihood from the observation model

Intuition. A forward map predicts measurements, while a noise model describes how observations fluctuate around those predictions. The likelihood connects the observed data to possible unknowns.

Mathematics. For y=𝒢h(u)+ηy=\mathcal G_h(u)+\eta with η∼𝒩(0,Γ)\eta\sim\mathcal N(0,\Gamma) independent of uu and fixed Γ\Gamma,

Φh(u;y)=12(y−𝒢h(u))⊤Γ−1(y−𝒢h(u)),πh(u∣y)∝e−Φh(u;y)π0(u).\Phi_h(u;y)=\tfrac12(y-\mathcal G_h(u))^\top\Gamma^{-1}(y-\mathcal G_h(u)),\qquad \pi_h(u\mid y)\propto e^{-\Phi_h(u;y)}\pi_0(u).

The subscript hh identifies a numerical forward approximation. Terms constant in uu can be omitted for MH. If a covariance depends on sampled parameters, its log determinant generally cannot be dropped.

Numerical example. Let u=(u1,u2)∼𝒩(0,I2)u=(u_1,u_2)\sim\mathcal N(0,I_2) and observe y=u1+u2+η=1y=u_1+u_2+\eta=1 with noise standard deviation 0.1. The log target, up to a constant, is

ℓ(u)=−12(u12+u22)−50(1−u1−u2)2.\ell(u)=-\tfrac12(u_1^2+u_2^2)-50(1-u_1-u_2)^2.

Both (0.5,0.5)(0.5,0.5) and (1,0)(1,0) fit the datum exactly; the prior assigns them different densities.

What problem does this solve? It turns a deterministic forward model plus uncertainty assumptions into a probability distribution that an MCMC algorithm can target. Incorrect noise scaling changes the posterior itself; a better sampler cannot fix it.

6.2 Sample the posterior and validate on a solvable case

Intuition. Use MCMC to explore all plausible unknowns consistent with the prior and data. Begin with a problem whose exact answer is available, then replace the forward map by the nonlinear or PDE model.

Mathematics. For a linear forward map AA and Gaussian prior 𝒩(m0,C0)\mathcal N(m_0,C_0),

C=(C0−1+A⊤Γ−1A)−1,m=C(C0−1m0+A⊤Γ−1y).C=(C_0^{-1}+A^\top\Gamma^{-1}A)^{-1},\qquad m=C(C_0^{-1}m_0+A^\top\Gamma^{-1}y).

For the numerical example, A=(1,1)A=(1,1) and Γ=0.01\Gamma=0.01 give

m=12.01(11),C=I2−12.01(1111).m=\frac1{2.01}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix},\qquad C=I_2-\frac1{2.01}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}.

Numerical example. Each posterior mean is approximately 0.4975. The covariance is approximately

C=(0.50249−0.49751−0.497510.50249).C=\begin{pmatrix}0.50249&-0.49751\\-0.49751&0.50249\end{pmatrix}.

The correlation is approximately -0.9901: the posterior is a narrow ridge. An isotropic random walk must respect the narrow direction and may move slowly along the ridge. Whitening or an appropriately chosen proposal covariance directly addresses this geometry.

What problem does this solve? It checks that the likelihood and sampler jointly recover the intended posterior. Agreement should be judged relative to estimated Monte Carlo error, not arbitrary decimal tolerances. In a PDE problem, HMC uses gradients of the implemented discretized potential; these often require adjoint calculations, while RWM and pCN require only potential evaluations.

6.3 Turn posterior draws into uncertainty quantification

Intuition. The data can determine some combinations of parameters accurately while leaving others uncertain. Analyze the quantities relevant to the scientific question, rather than only plotting individual parameter marginals.

Mathematics. For a quantity of interest q(u)q(u) and posterior draws u(n)u^{(n)}, compute

𝔼̂[q∣y]=1N∑nq(u(n)),ℙ̂(q>c∣y)=1N∑n𝟏{q(u(n))>c}.\widehat{\mathbb E}[q\mid y]=\frac1N\sum_n q(u^{(n)}),\qquad \widehat{\mathbb P}(q>c\mid y)=\frac1N\sum_n\mathbf1_{\{q(u^{(n)})>c\}}.

Empirical quantiles estimate credible intervals. To sample a future noisy observation, draw ηnew(n)\eta^{(n)}_{\mathrm{new}} from the observation-noise model and set ynew(n)=𝒢h(u(n))+ηnew(n)y^{(n)}_{\mathrm{new}}=\mathcal G_h(u^{(n)})+\eta^{(n)}_{\mathrm{new}}.

Numerical example. For the two-parameter problem,

𝔼[u1+u2∣y]≈0.9950,Var(u1+u2∣y)≈0.00995,\mathbb E[u_1+u_2\mid y]\approx0.9950,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(u_1+u_2\mid y)\approx0.00995,

whereas u1−u2∣y∼𝒩(0,2)u_1-u_2\mid y\sim\mathcal N(0,2) remains unchanged from the prior. The next noisy measurement of the sum has predictive variance 0.00995+0.01≈0.019950.00995+0.01\approx0.01995.

What problem does this solve? It separates identifiable scientific information from unresolved directions and converts posterior samples into means, credible intervals, event probabilities, and predictions. Each reported quantity needs its own accuracy assessment. More MCMC reduces simulation error; better data or modeling changes posterior uncertainty; mesh refinement addresses forward-approximation error.

A one-page summary

Goal. Compute I=∫fdπI=\int f\,d\pi for π(x)=π̃(x)/Z\pi(x)=\widetilde\pi(x)/Z, often with unknown ZZ and no direct sampler.

Monte Carlo. If XiX_i are IID from π\pi, estimate II by N−1∑f(Xi)N^{-1}\sum f(X_i). The strong law gives consistency; a finite-variance CLT gives MCSE about sf/Ns_f/\sqrt N. Four times as many independent draws halves the error. Posterior spread describes uncertainty about XX; MCSE describes numerical uncertainty about an estimated integral.

Importance sampling. Draw from qq and use weights w=π̃/qw=\widetilde\pi/q. Estimate II by ∑wf/∑w\sum wf/\sum w. It avoids knowing ZZ but can suffer severe weight degeneracy. A support mismatch cannot be repaired by more weighting.

Markov chains. P(x,A)P(x,A) specifies the next-state distribution. Invariance is πP=π\pi P=\pi. Detailed balance, π(dx)P(x,dz)=π(dz)P(z,dx)\pi(dx)P(x,dz)=\pi(dz)P(z,dx), is one sufficient route to invariance. Ergodicity supplies long-run exploration and a law of large numbers under the relevant conditions. Invariance, convergence, and efficiency are different requirements.

Algorithms. Metropolis accepts symmetric proposals with probability min(1,π̃(z)/π̃(x))\min(1,\widetilde\pi(z)/\widetilde\pi(x)). MH includes the reverse/forward proposal ratio. Gibbs samples full conditional distributions. HMC augments with momentum, integrates Hamiltonian dynamics using leapfrog, and corrects integration error. NUTS selects a valid variable-length Hamiltonian trajectory automatically.

Dependent-sample error. For a suitable stationary chain, τf=1+2∑k≥1ρf(k)\tau_f=1+2\sum_{k\ge1}\rho_f(k), ESSf≈N/τf\operatorname{ESS}_f\approx N/\tau_f, and MCSE≈sf/ESSf\operatorname{MCSE}\approx s_f/\sqrt{\operatorname{ESS}_f}. ESS depends on the estimand and can exceed NN under negative correlation. A CLT requires assumptions beyond an LLN.

Practice. Use log densities; retain rejected states; run multiple chains; use warmup for initialization and tuning. Inspect traces, rank-based split R̂\widehat R, bulk/tail ESS, and sampler warnings. Typical screening includes R̂<1.01\widehat R<1.01, but no finite diagnostic proves convergence. High acceptance does not imply efficient exploration. Compare ESS per time or solver cost. Extend runs to meet an MCSE target only after addressing exploration failures. Routine thinning is usually wasteful.

For inverse problems. Distinguish MCMC error, forward/discretization error, and model error. HMC may need adjoint gradients; pCN preserves a Gaussian reference prior and provides a natural path into function-space MCMC.

Exercise 4: Derive and observe slow Gibbs sampling (30 minutes)

Implement systematic two-coordinate Gibbs sampling for a centered bivariate Gaussian with unit marginal variances and correlations ρ=0,0.9,0.99\rho=0,0.9,0.99. Then whiten the target and repeat.

Exercise 5: Construct and validate HMC (45-60 minutes)

Implement fixed-length HMC for π=𝒩(m,C)\pi=\mathcal N(m,C) with non-diagonal CC, using U(x)=12(x−m)⊤C−1(x−m)U(x)=\tfrac12(x-m)^\top C^{-1}(x-m) and ∇U=C−1(x−m)\nabla U=C^{-1}(x-m). Explain how NUTS helps with trajectory length and what problems it does not solve.

Checks: exact mean is mm, exact covariance is CC. Exercise 1 solution

For the mean, MCSE = sqrt(0.2/N). For the exceedance probability, p = 1 − Φ((2−1.6)/sqrt(0.2)) ≈ 0.18555 and MCSE ≈ sqrt(p(1−p)/N). Increasing N tenfold reduces standard error by sqrt(10). A posterior interval describes X; a Monte Carlo interval describes numerical uncertainty in its estimated expectation.

Exercise 2 solution

The correct transition matrix is [[0.1,0.9],[0.225,0.775]]. Equilibrium off-diagonal flows both equal 0.18. Without the proposal correction, the reverse transition becomes 0.075, giving stationary probabilities (1/13,12/13).

Exercise 3 guidance

Retain rejected states and compare the mean and exceedance indicator separately. Small proposals can accept almost always while moving slowly. Keeping accepted states alone overrepresents states with higher probability of departure. Compare actual errors with MCSE only after assessing exploration.

Exercise 4 solution

Substitute the first conditional update into the second: X2,new = ρ² X2,old + ρ sqrt(1−ρ²) ξ1 + sqrt(1−ρ²) ξ2. The innovation variance is 1−ρ⁴. Hence the lag-k correlation is ρ^(2k), and τ = (1+ρ²)/(1−ρ²).

Use gradient C⁻¹(x−m). Leapfrog followed by momentum reversal is an involution up to floating-point error. Accept using the difference in total Hamiltonian. Check mean AND covariance: nearly periodic trajectories can estimate some quantities well and others poorly. Compare cost per effective draw, including all gradient evaluations.
